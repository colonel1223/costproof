CostProof

A causal measurement engine that verifies whether cloud cost optimisations actually saved money. What it is, how it works, what it proved, and how to explain it.

Where things stand right now

CostProof is a brand-new project, built from nothing this session. It lives at ~/Projects/costproof on your Mac. It is not yet pushed to GitHub.

TEA (Tariff Exposure Agent) has not been touched. It is your earlier project. It came up only because I found it while looking for somewhere to put this one, and noticed it has one commit and unfinished TODO markers. Not one file in it has been modified.

They are two separate repositories. Nothing has been merged, renamed, or overwritten.

01 What it is, in one sentence

CostProof takes a cloud billing feed and a log of cost-optimisation actions, and returns a defensible estimate of what each action actually saved — with a confidence interval, a validity check, and an audit trail.

It is not a dashboard. Every FinOps product on the market already tells you what you spent. CostProof is the layer underneath the savings claim that tells you whether the claim is true.

The analogy — memorise this one

A doctor gives a patient medicine when they have a fever. The fever goes down. Did the medicine work?

You can't tell. Fevers go down on their own. To know whether the medicine did anything, you need a comparison group: similar patients, same fever, who didn't get the medicine.

Cloud costs work identically. A team "fixes" a server when its cost spikes. The cost comes down. But costs come down on their own after a spike — that's what a spike is. Without a comparison group, you cannot tell a real saving from a fever breaking.

That comparison group is the entire product. Everything technical in the repository exists to build it correctly and to prove it was built correctly.

02 The problem it solves

Cloud is one of the largest controllable costs in a modern company, and the wasted share is rising:

Read rows one and four together. 63% of companies employ a team whose whole job is cutting cloud waste, and waste went up anyway. Either those teams don't work, or nobody can measure whether they work.

The FinOps Foundation's own 2026 survey contains a practitioner explaining the problem without realising what they're describing:

Verbatim, State of FinOps 2026

"Once you fix it, it's gone… how do we give developers credit for shift-left activities?"

They are saying: we intervened, the cost disappeared, and now we cannot observe what the cost would have been if we hadn't. That missing quantity has a name in economics — a counterfactual — and an entire toolkit built to estimate it. The industry has a measurement problem and doesn't know it already has a solution in a neighbouring field.

Why the current method is broken

Everyone computes savings as spend before − spend after. That silently assumes nothing else changed. Four things always do:

  • Traffic grows or shrinks — the baseline was never going to hold still
  • Seasonality — retail in November, education in September
  • Other deployments — engineering ships other things in your window
  • Mean reversion — the dangerous one

Mean reversion deserves its own sentence, because it is the whole game. Teams optimise the thing that just spiked. A spike is, by definition, an unusually high reading — and the next reading is lower on average whether or not you did anything. So before/after hands the team credit for a decline that was coming regardless.

03 How it works

STEP 1Ingest

Read billing data in FOCUS 1.2 — the open standard AWS, Azure and GCP all publish to.

STEP 2Find comparisons

For each optimised resource, select untouched resources whose costs moved the same way beforehand.

STEP 3Estimate

Difference-in-differences and synthetic control build the counterfactual and measure the gap.

STEP 4Check validity

Test whether the comparison group was legitimate. Refuse to report an estimate that fails.

STEP 5Score

Because true effects are known, grade every method on accuracy and honesty of its intervals.

Step 2 — comparison groups match on movement, not size

The donor selection ranks candidates by how closely their cost growth tracked the treated resource before the action. This matters: the assumption being relied on is about changes, not levels. A resource costing a tenth as much but moving in lockstep is a good control; one costing the same but drifting is a bad one.

Step 3 — the two estimators

Difference-in-differences. Compare the change in the treated resource to the change in the control group over the same window. The second subtraction cancels everything that hit both — seasonality, price changes, company-wide traffic, and mean reversion. Implemented as a regression with fixed effects for each resource and each day, which absorb permanent differences between resources and anything common to a given day.

Synthetic control. When no single resource is comparable, build an artificial one: a weighted blend of untouched resources, with weights chosen so the blend tracks the treated resource closely before the action. Weights must be non-negative and sum to one, which forces the blend to stay inside the range of real resources. If no valid blend exists, the method says so rather than extrapolating.

Step 4 — the validity check

DiD relies on an assumption called parallel trends: absent the action, treated and control would have moved together. This is untestable directly, but it has a testable implication — they should have moved together before the action. If they didn't, the design is broken and the estimate is discarded. Section 6 shows this filter actually working.

04 Why the data is simulated

Expect this question — have the answer ready

An interviewer will ask about the data. Say this before they ask. Volunteering a limitation reads as rigour; being caught by it reads as spin.

Two reasons, and the second is the real one.

First, no company will hand a student their cloud invoices. Billing data is commercially sensitive. That's the obvious reason and the weaker one.

Second — and this is the point — on real data you could never check whether the method works. The true saving is unobservable in the real world. That's the entire problem. So on real billing data you can run an estimator, but you can never grade it.

The simulator writes the answer key. Every one of the 117 optimisation actions has a known true effect built in, so each method can be scored on how close it gets. Thirty of those actions have a true effect of exactly zero — actions that did nothing, applied to resources that had merely spiked. Those thirty are the most valuable rows in the dataset, because any method that reports savings on them is manufacturing them.

Say it like this

"The data is simulated, deliberately. On real billing data the true saving is unobservable — that's the problem I'm solving. So I built a generator where I know the right answer for every action, which let me measure how accurate each method actually is. My claim isn't 'I saved a company two million dollars.' It's 'here's an estimator, and here's exactly how often it's right.' That's a testable claim. The other one isn't."

The simulator is not a toy. It reproduces real cloud economics: published AWS, Azure and GCP list prices; enterprise discounts; committed-spend amortisation; unused commitment; heavy-tailed resource sizes; weekly and annual seasonality; and a 10.6% untagged spend rate, because real estates never tag everything.

Critically, it assigns optimisations the way real teams do — to whatever just spiked. If actions were assigned randomly, before/after would work fine and there would be no problem to solve. Reproducing that bias faithfully is what makes the whole exercise mean anything.

06 The results

Estate: $8.9M annualised, 400 resources, 216,036 billing rows over 18 months, 117 optimisation actions (30 with a true effect of exactly zero).

Method RMSE Interval coverage False positives Power
Naive before/after (industry standard)0.23827%80%100%
DiD, cluster-robust errors0.19923%83%100%
DiD, randomization inference0.19991%13%91%
Synthetic control0.24484%0%48%

What each column means. RMSE — typical size of the error, in log points; smaller is better. Coverage — how often the 95% confidence interval actually contains the truth; should be 95%. False positives — how often the method declares a significant saving on one of the 30 actions that did nothing; should be 5%. Power — how often it detects a real effect.

The three findings, in order of how much they land

1. Before/after reports a significant saving on 80% of actions that did nothing. Not 5%. Eighty. Four times out of five, the industry standard manufactures a saving out of a spike that was reverting anyway. This is the number that makes a finance audience sit up.

2. Rows two and three are the same estimate. Identical arithmetic. Only the uncertainty calculation differs. Standard errors assume you have many treated units to average over; here there is exactly one treated resource, so they come out far too narrow and the intervals cover the truth 23% of the time. Replacing them with randomization inference — re-running the analysis pretending each untouched resource was the treated one, and using that spread as the real scale of uncertainty — takes coverage to 91% and drops false positives from 83% to 13%. Nothing about the economics changed. Only the honesty of the error bars.

3. The validity check works as a trust filter. Splitting the estimates by whether they pass the parallel-trends test:

GroupnRMSECoverage
Passes the validity check1030.15195.1%
Fails the validity check140.40264.3%

Estimates that pass have less than half the error and essentially perfect 95% coverage. The system knows which of its own answers to trust — and that was measured, not asserted. This is what makes it safe to put in front of a CFO: it refuses to answer when it can't answer well.

In dollars

On a portfolio whose true annual saving is $2,020,441, the industry method misstates the programme by $326,700 (16%). The identified estimator misstates it by $210,954 (10%) — and unlike the naive method, tells you honestly how uncertain it is.

07 How it was built — the process

This section matters more than the results. Interviewers ask how you think, and the failures are where that shows.

  1. Researched the problem before writing code. Read the FinOps Foundation and Flexera 2026 surveys, found the practitioner quote, and confirmed IBM's position (Apptio at $4.6B, plus Turbonomic and Kubecost).
  2. Built the data foundation on a real standard. Implemented the FOCUS 1.2 billing schema rather than inventing a CSV format, so anything the tool ingests works on real cloud exports.
  3. Built the simulator with an answer key, including endogenous treatment assignment so the bias being studied is genuinely present.
  4. Implemented DiD from scratch — the within transformation and the cluster-robust variance — then validated it against statsmodels on identical data. Agreement to machine precision (~1e-16) on both coefficient and standard error.
  5. Scored every method against the answer key and reported bias, RMSE, coverage, false-positive rate and power.

The two things that went wrong

Failure one — I blamed the estimator when the data window was wrong. The first full run gave 7% interval coverage and DiD barely beat naive. My instinct was that the estimator was broken. It wasn't. Waste was being fixed 20–75 days after it started, but the analysis window looked back 84 days — so the "before" period was a mixture of waste running and waste not yet started, and every method understated the effect. Fixing the window took RMSE from 0.60 to 0.199.

The lesson worth stating out loud When every method fails at once, suspect the data specification, not the methods.

Failure two — a broken variance estimate corrupted the test built on top of it. The validity check initially rejected 98.3% of designs, including ones that visibly looked fine. The test was built on the same standard errors that are invalid with one treated unit — each t-statistic inflated, so the test statistic inflated by roughly its square, so it rejected almost everything. Rebuilding the test on randomization inference took the pass rate to 88%, and the passing group turned out to have 95.1% coverage.

The lesson worth stating out loud A diagnostic inherits every weakness of the estimate it is built on.

30 seconds — the elevator version

"Companies spend millions on cloud and hire teams to cut that bill. When those teams claim they saved two million dollars, nobody can verify it — because verifying it means knowing what the bill would have been, and that number doesn't exist. I built the thing that estimates it, using causal inference methods from economics. The result: the method the industry uses today reports a real saving on eighty percent of actions that did nothing at all. Mine reports thirteen."

Two minutes — the Rule of 3 answer

"The problem: an industry spending hundreds of billions can't prove its own cost-saving work is worth anything. Their own survey has a practitioner saying 'once you fix it, it's gone — how do we give developers credit?' That's not a tooling gap. That's a missing counterfactual, described by a cloud engineer who doesn't know the term.

I did three things. I built a simulator where I knew the true effect of every action, so I could actually score my methods instead of just running them. I implemented difference-in-differences by hand — the fixed-effects transformation and the clustered variance — and validated it against statsmodels to machine precision. Then I graded four methods on accuracy, interval coverage, and false-positive rate.

Two things I got wrong taught me more than the result. My first run gave 7% coverage and I blamed the estimator — the real cause was that my analysis window reached back before the waste even started. When every method fails together, suspect the data, not the methods. Then my validity test rejected 98% of designs, because I'd built it on the same broken standard errors. A diagnostic inherits every weakness of the estimate underneath it."

The IBM-specific close

"IBM paid $4.6 billion for Apptio and owns Turbonomic and Kubecost. Turbonomic automatically takes these optimisation actions — thousands of them. Every one produces a savings claim nobody can substantiate. IBM sells the tools that generate the claims and owns nothing that verifies them. That's the gap I built into."

09 Questions you will get

Isn't this just A/B testing?

No — and the difference is the point. In an A/B test you choose who gets treated, at random. Here you can't: optimisations are applied to whatever spiked, and you can't randomly refuse to fix a broken server for the sake of an experiment. That's why this needs quasi-experimental methods, which reconstruct a comparison group after the fact instead of assigning one in advance.

Why not just use machine learning to predict what spend would have been?

That's the third estimator in the design hierarchy and it's the weakest, because it assumes the pre-period model is correctly specified and that nothing else changed at the same moment. A control group gets you that for free — anything that hit both groups cancels out without having to be modelled. Prediction is what you fall back to when no comparison group exists, not what you reach for first.

The data is fake, so what does this prove?

It proves the method works, which is the only thing that can be proven. On real data the true saving is unobservable — that's the problem being solved — so real data would let me run the estimator but never check it. Simulation is what makes the claim falsifiable rather than just plausible.

What would you do differently with real data?

The estimators run unchanged — that's why it's built on the FOCUS standard. Two things get harder. Comparison groups need screening for spillovers, because rightsizing one service can push load onto another. And treatment dates come from a change log or ticket queue, which is messier than a simulator's timestamps — a wrong date biases the estimate toward zero.

Why does an economics student build this rather than a software engineer?

An engineer sees a data pipeline and a dashboard, builds excellent versions of both, and the number at the end is still before-minus-after and still wrong. An economist recognises the shape immediately: treatment applied to some units and not others, outcomes over time, a counterfactual that has to be constructed. That's week one of econometrics. The contribution isn't inventing a method — it's noticing that a multi-billion-dollar industry problem was solved forty years ago in a different field.

What's the weakest part?

Spillovers. If optimising one resource shifts load onto a resource in the comparison group, the comparison group is contaminated and the effect is overstated. It's screened for but not eliminated. The second weakness is that the simulator encodes my own beliefs about how cloud spend behaves — if those beliefs are wrong in a way that flatters the estimator, the validation is optimistic.

10 What happens next

In build order, because each layer feeds the next:

  1. SQL warehouse (DuckDB) — star schema, bronze/silver/gold layers, cost allocation, and unit economics: cost per transaction and per AI inference. Answers "is this bill rising because we're wasteful or because we're growing," which half the industry can't answer.
  2. ML layer — spend forecasting, anomaly detection, and a classifier that separates genuine waste from benign growth. Reported with precision and recall.
  3. R cross-validation — re-estimate with CausalImpact and fixest. Two independent implementations agreeing is a real robustness claim.
  4. MCP server + RAG + watsonx agent — expose the analytics as tools an agent can call, retrieval over pricing docs and policy, and a human approval gate with a full audit trail. This is where the IBM certifications get demonstrated rather than listed.
  5. Excel business case — savings waterfall, programme NPV and IRR, sensitivity analysis. The artefact a finance stakeholder actually reads.
